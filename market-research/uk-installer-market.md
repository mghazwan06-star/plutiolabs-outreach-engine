# UK Installer Market: Research Base

**Every figure below is sourced and confidence-graded.** This document is the evidence base the ICP and outreach targeting were built against, not a sales deck. It's a research file, maintained as a living document and corrected when a better source turns up.

## How to read the confidence grades

| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[A]** | Official statistics: ONS, MCS, GOV.UK, Insolvency Service. Load-bearing. |
| **[B]** | Multiple independent commercial sources agreeing. Trust within roughly ±15%. |
| **[C]** | Single source or vendor-published marketing data. Directionally useful only. |
| **[D]** | A derived model built from A-C inputs, assumptions stated inline. |

The rule this document runs on: never treat a [C] figure as load-bearing. A single vendor-published stat that turns out to be inflated or self-serving can take down everything built on top of it if it's ever checked and found wrong.

## Market structure: who these businesses actually are

Source: ONS enterprise counts, SIC 43.22 (plumbing, heat and air-conditioning installation), 2025. **[A]**

| Employees | Firms | % of firms | % of turnover |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 5,880 | 12.7% | 2.3% |
| 1-9 (micro) | 38,140 | 82.1% | 42.4% |
| **10-49 (small)** | **2,160** | **4.6%** | **29.2%** |
| 50-249 | 245 | 0.5% | 18.9% |
| 250+ | 25 | 0.1% | 7.2% |

94.8% of the market has under 10 employees, holding under half the sector's total revenue: a very long tail of small operators. The 10-49 band, at just 4.6% of firms, holds nearly a third of the industry's turnover at an average of £3.35m each. Large enough to have a real marketing budget, small enough to have no in-house operations team.

Certified installer populations: MCS-certified contractors across all technologies exceed 5,250 **[A]**. Of those, over 4,000 hold solar PV certification **[A]**. 2,215 are active MCS heat pump installers **[A]**. Market concentration is low, highly fragmented, no dominant player, which matters for reachability: there's no gatekeeper controlling access to this population.

## Job economics

| Work type | Typical installed price | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Solar PV, domestic | £8,677 average | [B] |
| Air source heat pump | £8,000-15,000 before grant | [B]/[D] |
| Boiler replacement | £2,300 median quote | [B]/[D] |

Grant mechanics, worth stating precisely because it's easy to get backwards: the installer invoices the full contract value and claims the government grant on the customer's behalf. A heat pump customer might pay roughly £4,500 out of pocket while the installer's actual revenue on that job is £12,000. Heat pump work is genuinely the highest-value job type on this target list, not the lowest, a detail that inverts a common assumption about which trades pay best.

Job volumes, and why the mean is misleading. Solar PV averages 5.6 installs per installer per month nationally, but that figure is heavily skewed by a handful of national players doing thousands each. The median independent installer does closer to 2 to 4 a month. Modelling against the mean rather than the median would badly overstate what a typical target business is actually running.

## The installer's own P&L: why volume framing fails

Modelled for an 8-person solar installer doing 12 installs a month **[D]**.

| Line | Monthly |
|---|---|
| Revenue | £104,000 |
| Gross profit (~32%) | £33,000 |
| Marketing / lead buy | -£7,000 to -£12,000 |
| Other overhead | -£16,000 to -£22,000 |
| **Net profit** | **£3,000-£8,000** |

This single model is what reframed everything downstream. A firm already netting only £3,000 to £8,000 a month isn't looking for "more volume" as an abstract good. It's looking at every new cost line as a direct hit against a thin margin. And they already spend £7,000 to £12,000 a month on marketing. Anything that improves how much of that existing spend actually converts is a fundamentally different pitch to anything that asks for a new budget line. The reframe is protecting an existing spend, not adding to it.

## Lead economics: what a missed call actually costs

| Metric | Value | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Solar cost-per-lead, exclusive Meta Ads | £18-40 | [B] |
| Lead-to-sale close rate, UK domestic solar | 15-20% | [B] |

Every lost lead isn't a zero, it's a wasted £233 or more the business already spent to generate it. That's the single reframe underneath the whole leakage argument this pipeline is built to address: the value isn't creating new demand, it's recovering acquisition spend that's already been made.

## The leakage model: the one number with real academic provenance

Most stats in this space are vendor-published and inflated. One figure stands apart.

A lead is 21x more likely to qualify if contacted within 5 minutes versus 30 minutes. Dr James B. Oldroyd, MIT Sloan / InsideSales.com Lead Response Management Study (2007). Three years of data, six companies, 15,000+ leads, 100,000+ call attempts. Later summarised in Harvard Business Review, "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads" (2011). **[A]**

A genuine academic study with a real sample size and an independently reproducible methodology, the only stat in this document graded [A] rather than [B] or [C] despite being about lead response rather than an official statistic. Every other "missed call" figure circulating in this space is vendor-published and should be treated with proportionally less weight.

## Demand: is it actually there?

The question that could invalidate the entire research base: if these businesses are already at capacity, "more leads" is a burden, not a benefit, and the whole premise fails. The honest answer splits by vertical, because it's not one answer.

**Solar, strong, growing, economically driven [B].** Certified installs hit an all-time record in 2025 at +32% year-on-year. 71% of people who'd install solar cite cutting their bill as the reason. This is bill-driven demand tracking a real, recently-risen energy price cap, not a fashion trend, which makes it a more durable driver than sentiment-based demand would be.

**Air conditioning, the standout, most under-rated vertical [B].** Domestic AC installation requests surged +320% year-on-year during a 2026 heatwave, the highest figure ever recorded on a major UK trades platform. Only around 5% of UK homes currently have air conditioning while roughly half overheat in summer. And the shape of that demand fits this system unusually well: it arrives in concentrated bursts of days, exactly when an installer is physically least able to answer the phone. On a roof in 37°C heat during the exact week every caller is ready to buy immediately.

**Heat pumps, demand broken upstream, and no response-time system fixes it [A]/[B].** Only 28% of the public has heard of a heat pump. Running costs sit roughly 12% more expensive than gas on a standard electricity tariff, given the current gap between UK electricity and gas unit prices. A homeowner who's never heard of the technology doesn't generate an enquiry to rescue, and one who's done the running-cost arithmetic isn't lost to a slow callback, they're lost to the numbers. A genuine, sourced finding that inverted an earlier assumption about which vertical to prioritise.

**Boiler and plumbing, structural, non-discretionary [B].** Nobody chooses when a boiler fails. Demand doesn't need creating and doesn't swing with policy or weather, the steady ballast underneath the more volatile verticals above.

## Do installers actually want more demand?

The headline finding, from installers' own stated priorities in a 2026 industry survey: 60% cite lack of consumer demand as the number one barrier to heat pump uptake **[B]**. Not skills, not supply chain, not grant paperwork. Demand, in their own words.

But the labour side tells a different story. 72% of construction firms report being affected by a skilled-trades shortage, up from 61% just six months earlier **[A]**, and the sector needs roughly 41,200 additional workers annually through 2030 just to keep pace **[A]**.

Both are true at once, and they resolve into a single insight worth stating outright: the order book has room, skilled installation labour is tight, and the actual bottleneck sits somewhere neither of those, in the owner's own admin time. A dedicated 2026 survey of 140 UK tradespeople measured 5 hours 20 minutes a week spent on quoting, invoicing and payment-chasing, disproportionately in evenings and weekends **[B]**. Named research house, dated, sample described, the most defensible of several overlapping admin-burden figures found in this research. Converting a higher share of leads already paid for consumes zero install capacity. It's a yield problem, not a volume problem, and that distinction is the single most important reframe this research produced.

## The regulatory tailwind

Three converging government commitments, all [A]-graded.

Future Homes Standard: in force from March 2027, with a transition to March 2028. From that date, solar and heat pumps become mandatory on all new builds in England.

Warm Homes Plan: just under £15bn in capital funding, the largest UK home-energy-efficiency investment on record, targeting 5 million homes upgraded by 2030.

Boiler Upgrade Scheme: confirmed funded through 2029/30 at a £2.7bn budget, £7,500 standard grant per household.

The practical read: this market's core bottleneck is shifting over the next several years from finding customers to handling the volume without dropping any of it. A demand wave that's government-underwritten rather than speculative, which is a meaningfully different kind of tailwind to build a targeting strategy against.

## Risk: seasonality and insolvency

Every vertical in this target list has a trough. Solar and AC slow through winter, boiler/heating work peaks in exactly that window. A single-vertical client feels any recurring cost hardest during their own trough, which is also when churn risk peaks, worth planning around rather than discovering.

Construction sits among the UK's most insolvency-exposed sectors: 3,805 construction insolvencies in the twelve months to mid-2026, 17% of all recorded cases where an industry was identified **[A]**. A real, sourced risk factor for any business model built around ongoing relationships with small UK trades firms, not a hypothetical one.
