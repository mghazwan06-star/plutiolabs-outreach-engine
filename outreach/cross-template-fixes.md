# Cross-Template Fixes: Eight Things Learned Writing Five Templates

Five email templates (A, A-v2, A-lite, B, D) each went through the same cycle: audit the real leads that would receive it, stress-test the copy against every one of them, rewrite, re-verify. Eight of the bugs found during that process were not specific to any single template's copy. They were structural, because A-lite, B, A-v2 and D were all built as siblings of Template A's original skeleton and could carry the same defect until each one was checked individually. This is the checklist that resulted, and it's written up in full because three of these bugs were only found by reading finished, already-shipped copy end to end as a real recipient would, not by any checklist pass, and that distinction is the actual lesson.

## The eight fixes

**1. The complaint-mismatch bug, the most severe of the eight.** Never assert a specific complaint category ("the phone rings and you miss it," "every call you miss") immediately after quoting a lead's real complaint text. The real complaint is frequently about something else entirely: late quotes, workmanship, no-shows, follow-up. Checked against 22 real leads on the template that names complaints most: only 32% of complaints were actually about a missed call. Asserting "call" as the cause read as a factual mismatch next to the other 68%, precisely the kind of error a recipient reading about their own business would catch instantly. Fixed with wording that points back at whatever the complaint quote just said ("every time that happens," "the same problem") instead of restating an assumed cause. Confirmed present, and fixed, on a second template too, where the mismatch rate on real data was worse than the first: 100%.

**2. No named mechanism in social proof.** The case-study line states background and result only: a similar business, the same problem, a number, a timeframe. It never says how the result happened ("automated," "set up a system," etc). Naming the mechanism is the exact sentence that turns evidence into a pitch, and a skeptical, agency-burned reader disengages the moment a case study starts explaining itself rather than just stating what happened.

**3. No guarantee or risk-reversal language.** "If it doesn't book you a job, I'll run it free until it does" reads as a generic sales trope to this specific audience, cut entirely rather than reworded, because no phrasing of a guarantee escaped sounding like every other agency promise this audience has already been burned by. The call-to-action carries the low-commitment framing on its own instead.

**4. De-duplicate trade-word repetition.** A trade name mentioned once, then a bare "trade scene" phrase reused a sentence or two later, produces visibly repeated language for certain trades (a heat-pump lead reading "heat pump... heat pump" back to back). Solved with a de-duplicated scene variable used wherever a second trade mention would otherwise land near the first.

**5. Word count discipline, measured, not eyeballed.** Target sat at 90 to 115 words, up to 125 as a hard ceiling on some templates. Every template got rendered against the full real spread of eligible leads, longest competitor names, longest city names, both trades, and the actual min/max reported, rather than judged against one hand-picked example.

**6. Every sentence needs a verb.** One template shipped with a label-and-colon construction ("Your customers: '[quote]'") that reads as a fragment, not a sentence, with no indication of who's speaking. This predates the redesign entirely and survived a full rewrite unnoticed, because the rewrite checked word count, guarantee language and mismatch specifically, not basic sentence completeness on the one line that wasn't otherwise touched. Caught only by reading the finished copy end to end after it had already gone out, not by any structured pass. Fixed to a verb-based construction matching the pattern already used correctly elsewhere ("your customers say why," "your reviews mention it").

**7. Every word a call-to-action references needs a real antecedent already in the email.** Two templates shipped asking "how could that gap close?" while the word "gap" never appeared anywhere earlier in the body, only raw numbers, leaving the reader to do the labelling themselves. The fix: read backwards from the call-to-action and check every noun it references has already been said on the page. Cheap to fix, usually a one or two word insertion, but easy to miss because the call-to-action is typically the last thing written and the first thing skimmed past on a re-read.

**8. Never assert a premise the reader can simply disagree with, including one hiding inside an otherwise well-grounded sentence.** This was the hardest of the eight to catch, and it shipped wrong twice before landing right. One template asserted that enquiries get missed "while the reader is out on a job," a flat claim about the reader's own behaviour with nothing backing it. The first attempted fix wrongly assumed two sibling templates were safe from the same issue, on the reasoning that their version of the same line sat next to a real, quoted complaint, so it must be grounded. Checking all real complaints for those two templates against the paired physical-scene phrase found zero connected to being physically busy on a job. The real complaints were about missed appointments, late quotes, workmanship, follow-up, several of them office or admin failures, the literal opposite of "I was on a roof." A real citation sitting next to an unrelated claim had made the whole sentence look grounded by association, when only half of it actually was. The general rule that came out of it: check every clause in a sentence independently. A real citation nearby does not automatically ground the rest of the sentence sitting next to it.

The same root problem showed up a second time in a call-to-action that asserted the reader has a specific problem and asked them to confirm it, rather than asking whether it applies. That version was replaced twice before landing on the right shape, because the first replacement fixed the assertion but introduced a new problem: it read as an offer to personally fix something for the reader, which is the same sales-trope pattern rule 3 above exists to prevent. The eventual fix followed a formula already present elsewhere in the playbook and, notably, never actually used yet by any template until this point: state the general truth, then ask whether it applies, and leave a genuine way to answer either way.

## The standing lesson

A call-to-action can pass every mechanical check, correctly grounded, no fabricated numbers, right word count, right offer tier, and still fail on tone or premise. Tone failure looks like implying the sender will personally do something to the reader's business rather than offer information. Premise failure looks like asserting a problem the reader may not actually have. Neither shows up in a checklist. Both only show up when the finished copy gets read the way an actual first-time recipient would read it, which is why the last three of these eight fixes were all found that way rather than through any structured audit pass, and why every new template now gets that same plain read-through before shipping, not just the structured checks.
